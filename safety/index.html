AI safety and governance

Safety is a product decision.

Most of the hard calls in a product are made long before a safety team sees it: what data is collected, what the system is allowed to do, and what counts as harm. This page sets out the techniques I use to make those calls, each shown as a pair: the idea behind it, and where it has been put into practice. They run from a browser used by billions, through a dating app's safety team, to the AI systems I build today.

What I believe

Five beliefs underneath the techniques.

Ask “private from whom?” before “how private?”

“I want privacy” is not yet a requirement. It becomes one when it says who the user wants privacy from, and whom they are willing to trust to get it.

Write the never list before the feature list

What a system must never do is agreed first, and each line is then made true by design, rather than by an instruction the system might ignore.

Capability and risk are the same dial

Every permission that makes an AI system more useful also makes it more dangerous. The job is to turn the dial deliberately, one step at a time, on evidence.

Some risks are managed, not eliminated

Where a risk cannot be removed, it is reduced by design, watched continuously, and owned by a named person who accepts in writing what remains.

Measure both kinds of error, or you are only measuring one

A safety system that is judged only on what it catches will quietly punish the people it wrongly flags. What it misses and what it gets wrong are both counted, from the start.

Frame the problem

Private from whom?

Turn “I want privacy” into a requirement by asking who the threat is and whom the user trusts.
The idea

Privacy cannot be designed in the abstract. The same feature can protect a user who trusts the provider but fears a third party, and expose one who wants privacy from the provider itself.

In practice

Used to untangle how Incognito sessions should be handled on the server at Chrome, and to decide where each kind of data may go in Job Scout, whose most private reading happens on the local PC.

Frame the problem

Privacy as a hygiene factor

For most users privacy has to clear a bar, not win a competition.
The idea

Privacy work is usually revenue-negative, or neutral at best, and few users choose a product for it. It is the reason they leave, not the reason they join, so the case for it has to be made on trust and reputation.

In practice

The strategic argument for privacy investment in Chrome, at a time when competing browsers had begun to position themselves on privacy.

Frame the problem

Name the trade between safety and privacy

The two are sometimes aligned and sometimes opposed; the trade is made explicitly.
The idea

Protecting people often means knowing more about what they do, which costs them privacy. Neither side wins by default, so the trade is named, argued and decided rather than left to whoever shouts loudest.

In practice

Browser protection against dangerous sites works better the more it is told about the pages a user visits: a clear case of privacy traded for safety, with no single right answer.

Frame the problem

Safety and quality as one problem

Fix the harm and you often fix the experience too.
The idea

Harmful behaviour and poor experience often share a cause. Treating them as separate problems splits the effort and the data.

In practice

At Bumble, a safety team and a team working on the quality of conversations were tackling the same behaviour from two sides. I brought them together to work on it as one problem.

Gate and govern

A launch review with three verdicts

Yes; yes, but with required changes; or blocked.
The idea

A review gate is only as good as the people in it. With engineers in the room it becomes a technical review rather than a policy sign-off, and a middle verdict, “yes, but”, turns it from a veto into a design partner.

In practice

The privacy review every Chrome launch had to clear, with early consultation offered to teams that spotted a risk themselves, and escalation to senior leadership when a block was contested.

Gate and govern

Offer the privacy-preserving route

The “yes, but” answer: meet the need with less data.
The idea

A review that only says no teaches teams to avoid it. A review that offers a better design, even a harder one to build, gets used.

In practice

Matching a user's contacts by comparing hashed values, rather than uploading the contact list as it stands: heavier engineering, much better privacy.

Gate and govern

Turn policy into product structure

A policy position only protects people once a team and a backlog own it.
The idea

Safety policy written in the abstract changes little. It has to become teams, owners, priorities and features before it reaches a user.

In practice

The product safety strategy across Bumble and Badoo, turning policy intent into a concrete product and team structure, with executive sponsors aligned across two offices.

Gate and govern

Human gates before anything is built

Nothing is built until the boundaries are checked and the tests agreed.
The idea

The cheapest place to stop an unsafe feature is before it exists. A definition of ready that includes the boundaries makes that check routine rather than heroic.

In practice

In every build, the product owner signs off refined stories, with their tests and boundary checks, before Claude, as an AI coding agent, writes any code.

Gate and govern

A paper trail a reviewer would ask for

Decisions, risks and limits written down, so the reasoning outlives the conversation.
The idea

Governance depends on being able to show why a decision was made and what risk was accepted. Recognised frameworks give that record a shape that reviewers already know.

In practice

Job Scout carries a numbered decision log, a system card, a threat model and a risk register organised by the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

Gate and govern

Managed, not eliminated

Treat prompt injection the way phishing is treated: reduced by design, watched, and owned.
The idea

No AI provider has solved the problem of models being steered by what they read, and the major ones say so. Waiting for a fix means nothing starts; pretending there is one is worse.

In practice

The governance model I propose to leadership teams introducing AI agents: a named executive owner accepts the remaining risk in writing, thresholds are set before anything is built, and failures are reported alongside successes.

Gate and govern

Give security the design, not a veto

Ask a scoped question with a deadline, not an open one.
The idea

Asked an open question with no scope, no deadline and no decision rights, the only safe answer a security team can give is no. Overruling them fails too, because an overruled team designs nothing.

In practice

The request I recommend leadership teams put to their security team: design the safest set-up for a named list of processes, state the risk that cannot be removed, and deliver in weeks, so that management can decide whether to accept it.

Contain by design

The never list

What the system must never do, written first and enforced by design.
The idea

A list of what a system must never do is shorter, clearer and easier to test than a list of what it should do. Each line is then made true in code, not left to an instruction.

In practice

Job Scout never applies, replies or contacts anyone on the user's behalf. The task app never writes back to its sources, never deletes and never merges on its own.

Contain by design

The Rule of Two

Private data, untrusted content, the ability to act: never all three unsupervised.
The idea

An AI session can reasonably run unattended with at most two of the three. With all three, a single hidden instruction in something it reads can turn it against its user.

In practice

Used to decide which parts of a build may run as an agent and which stay as ordinary code, and the basis of the classification I use with organisations deciding what may run unattended.

Contain by design

Data class decides where it runs

How sensitive the data is decides which deployment is acceptable.
The idea

Where a model runs decides who can see the data and what it costs. Setting each class of data against each deployment option makes the right choice for a piece of work plain, and it is rarely the same twice.

In practice

The deployment and exposure matrix I use with organisations, from public material that can go anywhere to customer personal data that belongs to a separate decision entirely.

Contain by design

Text from strangers is data, never instructions

Untrusted text is fenced off, cleaned and capped before a model sees it.
The idea

A model cannot reliably tell its user's instructions from instructions hidden in what it reads. So untrusted text is marked as data, stripped of instruction-like lines and limited in length.

In practice

Every job advert and email Job Scout reads is treated this way, and anything removed is flagged for the user to see.

Contain by design

Isolation and least privilege

Each part gets only the access its job needs, and some data no AI ever reaches.
The idea

The less a component can see and do, the less harm a mistake or an attack can cause. Where untrusted content meets private data, the reader is isolated from everything else.

In practice

Job Scout's mail access is read-only and its credentials are encrypted. Adverts and emails are read by a model on the local PC with no tools and no internet access. The task app reads every source read-only.

Contain by design

Code checks the AI

Nothing a model produces is written anywhere until ordinary code has checked it.
The idea

Models are used to interpret, draft and triage; deterministic code validates what they produce and keeps the record. An AI agent is used only where isolation, independence or on-demand use calls for one.

In practice

In Job Scout, an independent reviewer must quote the career record for every claim it accepts in a draft CV, and code confirms each quote exists before anything is saved.

Contain by design

Authority is earned

Shadow, assisted, delegated, extended: one step at a time, and back a step after an incident.
The idea

A system starts by watching, then suggests, then acts within bounds, and only then is given more. Each step is earned on evidence, and a confirmed incident steps it back automatically.

In practice

The autonomy ladder in the method. In Job Scout: read-only first, a pause switch on the board, and caps on how much a run can do, which also cap its cost.

Measure and test

Relative signals over absolute thresholds

When normal behaviour looks like harm, measure change rather than level.
The idea

A single score with a fixed threshold works only when normal use scores low. Where it does not, the signal is in the difference between people and in how fast things change.

In practice

Bumble's proactive toxicity detection, which looked at the gap in toxicity between the two people in a conversation and how quickly it was rising, because flirting scores as toxic.

The Bumble story →
Measure and test

Measure both kinds of error

Count what the system wrongly flags as carefully as what it misses.
The idea

False positives fall on innocent users and erode trust; false negatives leave harm in place. Each needs its own route to be found and counted.

In practice

At Bumble, a wrongly flagged message could be disputed from within the chat and went to human review, while missed cases surfaced through the existing reporting system.

The Bumble story →
Measure and test

Red team and independent review

Attack your own system, have it checked by something that did not build it, and set the bar first.
The idea

A builder grading its own work finds what it expects to find. Pass marks are set before the results are in, so a result cannot be argued with afterwards.

In practice

Job Scout stopped all ten hostile inputs its red team tried and read 21 of 22 test adverts correctly. A separate agent reviews code it did not write, and every finding in the task app's QA pass was fixed with a test that failed first.

In depth

Three stories behind the techniques.

Google · Chrome Privacy

The review every launch had to clear

Launch review · the “yes, but” · privacy as a hygiene factor

The problem. Every Chrome launch had to pass a privacy review before it shipped. Engineers sat in the review alongside product, so it tested the design rather than the paperwork, and it returned one of three verdicts: yes; yes, but with required changes; or blocked. Teams could also come early, when they had spotted a risk themselves.

The tension. The hardest calls were rarely about engineering. Blocking third-party cookies in Incognito was about two weeks of build. At Chrome's scale, though, it meant modelling the effect on advertising revenue from scratch, and months of competition-law review, because restricting a cookie that rival ad businesses depended on carried risks of its own.

The decision. It was held back, then shipped in 2020, once another major browser had made the same move and the change had become the industry norm.

Safety and privacy work is mostly the work around the build: the stakeholders, the money and the law.

Bumble · Safety & Integrity

When flirting looks like abuse

Relative signals · both kinds of error · safety and quality as one problem

The problem. Bumble's first proactive safety feature was designed to step in while a conversation was turning, rather than after a report. It used a beta toxicity-scoring model from Google, which rates a message between 0 and 1.

The tension. The obvious design, flagging anything above a threshold, fails on a dating app. People are meant to flirt and be direct, and flirting scores as toxic. Research also showed that much of the harm came from people unaware of how they were coming across, so the feature was as much about the quality of conversation as about safety.

The decision. Two relative signals replaced the absolute score: the gap in toxicity between the two people, and how quickly it was rising. Wrongly flagged messages could be disputed in the chat and went to human review; missed ones surfaced through user reports. A separate team working on conversation quality was brought together with the safety team, and the problem was tackled as one.

The right measure depends on what normal looks like for the people using the product.

Own build · Job Scout

Guardrails on a personal AI agent

Never list · containment · red team

The problem. Job Scout runs a job search every morning on a private mailbox and career record. It reads alerts and adverts, scores each role and drafts a CV and letter for the best few: private data and text written by strangers, in the same system.

The tension. Every useful step adds risk. An agent that could apply, reply or follow up would save the most time, and would also be the most dangerous thing to leave running.

The decision. The never list came first: it never applies, replies or contacts anyone, and every decision stays with the user. Adverts and emails are read on the local PC by a model with no tools and no internet access, and only a checked summary leaves the machine. Claims in each draft are verified against the career record by code. Then it was attacked: all ten hostile red-team inputs were stopped.

Decide what it must never do before deciding what it should do.

Try it

Is this safe to run unattended?

A language model cannot reliably tell an instruction from its user apart from one hidden in something it is reading. The major AI labs say so themselves, and none describes the problem as solved. Where the model runs changes who can see the data and what it costs; it changes nothing about this risk.

What works is containment, known as the Rule of Two. An AI session can reasonably run unattended if it has no more than two of three properties: access to private data, exposure to content it did not write, and the ability to act or communicate with the outside world. In practice: Job Scout
