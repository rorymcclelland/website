Walkthrough

RFI Respond, screen by screen

RFI Respond answers procurement questionnaires from an organisation's own knowledge base. This walkthrough follows one response through the tool and marks four things on each screen: what the AI does, where a person decides, the guardrails, privacy, and security design, and how each part was tested. Use the filter to follow one thread at a time; select a numbered marker to find its note.

Download the case study ↓View the code on GitHub ↗

The screens are from the build of 4 November 2026 (Sprint 24), taken by the browser tests on the synthetic demo data in the repository. Company names are fictional, and the drafts were recorded from a real run of the pipeline.

01

What needs you today

Everyone lands on Home: what needs them today, the responses they own, and what the organisation knows. A visitor can try the whole job on a sample questionnaire of their own.

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    Human in the loop

    At risk, with the reason. A response owner sees her responses that are at risk or overdue first, with why: here, due 6 November with nine questions still to do. Each response belongs to one named person, who decides what is sent.

  2. 2
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    A try is never learned. Try it makes the visitor their own response from a short questionnaire from a fictional company, drafted with the real AI. The database learns nothing from a try, counts none of its questions as gaps, refuses to let a try be handed to someone else, and allows five tries a person a day.

  3. 3
    How it was tested

    Tried with the real AI on every staging run. Each run on the staging copy drafts a try with the real models and checks it end to end: the holds and their reasons, bulk approval, the download, and nothing learned.

  4. 4
    Human in the loop

    What the organisation knows, at a glance. How many answers there are, in which language, and how many are reviewed and current, waiting for review, or machine translations still to check.

  5. 5
    AI capability

    Ask it a question. Anyone can type a question as a customer would and see the answer the tool would draft, with its confidence and sources, through the same pipeline as a response. Nothing about the question is saved.

02

A response, and what each question needs

A response's page reads like its card: its owner, due date and progress, its files, and three stages: Questions, With experts, and Send. The questions are grouped by what they need, one reason each.

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    Human in the loop

    One owner, one due date, one progress bar. The header speaks for the whole response, across every file a customer sent: since Sprint 24 a response's questions are one list, filtered by file if wished, approved in bulk and marked as sent once.

  2. 2
    Human in the loop

    The job in three stages. Questions, then the colleagues asked to help, then sending: each stage says what it holds, so the owner can see where the work stands without opening a question.

  3. 3
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Held, and each says why. Questions under Needs a person are never approved in bulk. Each card shows one reason first, in plain words, so nothing is held without an explanation.

  4. 4
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Pricing is a person's. Pricing is never drafted, never bulk-approved, and never learned into the knowledge base, because prices go stale and are the organisation's to set.

  5. 5
    How it was tested

    The gate was tested before it was trusted. Rules and a classifier together caught 100 of 100 pricing and legal questions in the evaluation set. 7 of 70 harmless lookalikes were held by mistake, an error that falls on the side of a person.

  6. 6
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Legal questions are always individual. A question that would commit the organisation legally or contractually is flagged and reviewed one at a time, whatever its score.

  7. 7
    AI capability

    The tool says when it does not know. When the knowledge base does not answer a question, nothing is drafted, and the card says so rather than producing something plausible.

  8. 8
    Human in the loop

    Asked of a colleague. Questions the owner cannot answer are assigned to a colleague, suggested from their stated expertise and from who wrote or approved similar answers (screen 5).

03

Ready to approve, in bulk, with a name on every decision

Drafts nothing holds are listed most confident first, with the bulk bar. Approved answers carry who approved them, when, and how.

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    Human in the loop

    Bulk approval, as a recorded decision. The owner can approve every draft at or above a chosen confidence in one action. It is still a person's decision, recorded with who made it, when, and at what threshold.

  2. 2
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    What bulk approval can never include. Pricing, legal, and translated questions; anything not fully checked; drafts citing an unreviewed, overdue, or replaced entry; a "partly" verdict; and anything without calibrated confidence or a draft. The database enforces this, and checks each cited entry again when bulk approval runs.

  3. 3
    AI capability

    Japanese questions, answered from English knowledge. Matching works across languages: the right entry came back in the top five for every Japanese test question asked against English entries. The draft is written in Japanese.

  4. 4
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Translations are always reviewed. A translated draft is labelled as such and never bulk-approved; a person accepts it one at a time.

  5. 5
    How it was tested

    Calibration, checked out of sample. Five-fold cross-validation gave a calibration error of 0.047, and no near miss (on topic, but not answered) reached the 80% bulk line: 0 of 33.

  6. 6
    Human in the loop

    Who approved it, when, and how. Every accepted answer records the person, the date, and the method: here, a bulk approval at the 95% threshold.

  7. 7
    AI capability

    Confidence that means what it says. The figure is calibrated on the evaluation set: the measured chance the knowledge base answers the question. It is shown as "99%+" at the top, never as certainty.

  8. 8
    AI capability

    Similar is not the same as answered. Raw similarity is shown alongside (63% here), because an entry on the same topic is not necessarily an answer. That is why there is a checker.

04

A question the model is not allowed to answer

A pricing question is caught before drafting. There is no draft to approve, and the checker explains why the knowledge base does not cover it anyway.

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Held before drafting. The gate runs before the drafter, so a pricing question never reaches it. The card says who answers: a person.

  2. 2
    How it was tested

    Attacked with gate evasion. The red team tried to get pricing questions past the gate, in English and Japanese, by disguising or instructing around them. All were held.

  3. 3
    AI capability

    A plain reason, even for a "no". A separate Claude call reads the matched entries and says whether they answer the question, yes, partly, or no, with a reason a person can check.

  4. 4
    AI capability

    The entries that were considered. The closest entries and their similarity are listed, so a person can see what the tool looked at before deciding.

05

A colleague's question, answered on one screen

A specialist sees the questions they were asked in Assignments, and answers, approves, or hands one back without opening the response.

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    Human in the loop

    Who asked, for which response, by when. Each question arrives with the response it belongs to, its due date, and the person who asked.

  2. 2
    AI capability

    What the tool already knows. The colleague sees the tool's confidence and the checker's reason, here that the knowledge base gives no figure, so they know why they were asked.

  3. 3
    Human in the loop

    Their answer is the decision. Someone asked may write and approve the answer. The owner sees it on the response at once and can use it, change it, or decide otherwise.

  4. 4
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Each person sees their own work. Who may read or change what is enforced in the database for every role, so the rules hold even if someone calls the API directly. An answer is saved only while the question is still to do.

  5. 5
    How it was tested

    Seen failing when broken. This is a step of the end-to-end browser test. When the colleague's Approve was broken on purpose on a throwaway branch, the test failed here, naming the step.

06

Sent, and the record of what was sent

The tool never sends anything to the customer. Downloading produces the files the owner sends; Mark as sent records it, and is the moment the knowledge base learns.

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    A record, not a draft. Once a response is marked as sent, no file can be added to it, and the page says why: a later questionnaire is a new response. The database refuses it too, even mid-send.

  2. 2
    Human in the loop

    A change reopens it. Changing an answer after sending is recorded in the audit log, and the response must be marked as sent again before it counts as sent.

  3. 3
    Human in the loop

    Marked as sent by a person. Mark as sent says beforehand exactly what it will do, then records it with the person's name and the time, and closes any open assignments.

  4. 4
    Human in the loop

    Provenance in every file. Each download records how every answer was produced (drafted by a model, edited, or written by a person), with which model and prompt version, and who approved it.

  5. 5
    How it was tested

    Spreadsheet formula injection, tested. Excel cells are written as text, and CSV cells that start with a formula character are neutralised, so a planted formula never runs. A red-team case tried it.

07

What the organisation knows

The knowledge base is a place of its own: what it holds and in what state, what it could not answer in recent responses, and what it learned.

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    Human in the loop

    Every answer says where it came from. Imported, added by hand, or learned from a response, with who approved it and when it is next due for review.

  2. 2
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    A control against poisoning. Because the knowledge base learns, it is the obvious target for a bad answer. A member's addition goes in at once, but a draft citing it is never bulk-approved until an admin reviews it.

  3. 3
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Machine translations, checked. Answers translated by machine are counted until a person checks them, and drafts from them are always reviewed one at a time.

  4. 4
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Nothing stays current by default. Answers fall due for review 12 months after they were last checked; drafts citing an overdue answer are held from bulk approval.

  5. 5
    AI capability

    Gaps: what it could not answer. Questions in recent responses that the knowledge base did not answer are listed with where each stands, so the next questionnaire starts from better material. Tries never count.

  6. 6
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Nothing overwritten unseen. A proposed change to an existing answer waits for review, and until then the answer stays as it is.

  7. 7
    AI capability

    Learned from each response. When a response is marked as sent, edited and person-written answers become entries linked to the response they came from. Pricing is never learned.

  8. 8
    How it was tested

    The replacement threshold was measured. A sweep chose the similarity above which an answer may replace an entry: wrong replacements came to 1.9% of test questions (7 of 363), against a bar of 2%.

08

One answer's story

Every answer has a page of its own: its history, where it has been used, its other languages, and any change waiting for review.

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    AI capability

    Find what was learned. Browse every answer by state, language, and where it came from, including those learned from responses.

  2. 2
    Human in the loop

    Its source, and its next review. The page names the document an answer came from and the date it falls due for review.

  3. 3
    Human in the loop

    Admins decide, nothing is deleted. An admin marks an answer still correct, edits it, or retires it. A retired answer can be restored, with its history.

  4. 4
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    A proposed change, side by side. An answer learned from a response that would replace this one is shown in full beside it, and changes nothing until an admin approves it.

  5. 5
    Human in the loop

    Traced to a person. The proposal names the response it came from and who approved the answer there.

09

Cost, limits, and review

Admins see what the AI costs and the limits that stop spend running away. (The demo's recorded calls fall in an earlier month, so this month shows none.)

Swipe the screenshot sideways to see all of it.

  1. 1
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Admins only. This page, and every admin action, is restricted by the database's access rules, which are tested as real users for every role.

  2. 2
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    A monthly budget. Processing stops for everyone when the month's estimated spend reaches the budget. Each question reserves its cost before it runs, so the cap holds under load.

  3. 3
    AI capability

    Every call priced. Claude Haiku 5.5 checks, classifies, and drafts; voyage-4, or a local model, matches by meaning. Every call is recorded with its tokens and cost, by person and by use.

  4. 4
    Guardrails, privacy, and security

    Daily limits per person. Questions processed, entries indexed, and Word documents read by AI each have a daily limit per person.

  5. 5
    How it was tested

    Caps and roles, tested in the database. 438 database tests run every role against the access rules, approvals, learning, review, and caps, as real signed-in users.

10

How it was tested

The green notes above show where each part was tested. These are the tests behind them. Every figure is an estimate on small synthetic sets, with a 95% interval in the repository's evaluation reports.

Red team

Prompt injection, 37 attacks

Questions and knowledge-base entries are text written by other people, so they are passed to the model as data, never as instructions. The red team planted instructions in 32 questions (each with a clean twin) and 5 entries.

false claimsprompt leaksscore manipulationlink insertionfake delimiterscitation forgerysource overriderole playencoded instructionsapproval spoofingemotional pressurefake conversation turnsjudge manipulationgate evasionscript injectionformula injectionreply floodingJapanese variants
Result. One injection moved the checker on a near miss from "no" to "partly". The drafter refused, so there was nothing to approve, and since Sprint 7 "partly" is never bulk-approved. A second was counted at first but was the drafter refusing a false claim; the scoring rule was narrowed after the run, and recorded as such.
Planted claims

Does the faithfulness judge catch a lie?

Drafts were judged faithful to their sources, but a judge that has only seen faithful drafts proves little. So unsupported claims were planted into 95 faithful drafts to see whether it would notice.

Result. Caught 95 of 95, with no false alarms on the 37 unchanged drafts. The plants came from four fixed rules, so a model's own mistakes may be harder to spot.
Hostile uploads

Files are treated as untrusted

Spreadsheets and Word documents are opened in the browser with size and decompression limits. The tests include a zip bomb, a macro-enabled .docm file, a file renamed to .docx, and a script tag hidden in the text.

Result. All refused or kept as plain text. Four Word documents carrying planted instructions changed nothing in the questions found.
Access rules

Every role, as a real user

Every rule on who may read or change what lives in the database, so it holds even with the public key in an attacker's hands. CI also searches the built app for any key that should not be there.

Result. 438 database tests, run against row-level security as real signed-in users, alongside 1,173 unit tests.
Browser tests

Every agreed case, as a person would use it

Since Sprint 20 the test cases agreed before each sprint run as browser tests on every push: on a local copy, and on a hosted staging copy where the real models draft a sample questionnaire. A separate AI agent checks that the tests prove the cases. Each sprint is signed off by the product owner from a page of recordings, and the live demo deploys only after his approval.

Result. 37 browser tests, among them the whole job end to end, from upload to the colleague's answer learned. Broken on purpose at one step, it failed there and named it.
Independent review

Code reviewed by something that did not write it

From Sprint 2 onwards, a separate AI agent that had not seen the reasoning reviewed each sprint's changes for security and correctness.

Result. In Sprint 24 it found two medium findings: a file or a question added while a response was being marked as sent could leave it called sent with work still to do. Both were fixed and tested with two database connections at once. Findings are fixed with a test, or recorded as a residual and added to the backlog.
Calibration and consistency

Measured, with what did not pass

Every published figure has a 95% interval and comes from an evaluation that can be re-run with one command.

Result. Calibration error 0.047 and 0 of 33 near misses at the bulk line. The checker still changes its verdict for 7.2% of questions when asked again, against a bar of 5%; that residual is accepted because only 1.7% crossed the bulk line.
