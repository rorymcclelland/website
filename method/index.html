The method

Redesigning a business around AI

Most organisations arrive at AI in much the same way. A few people start using an assistant on their own initiative, one or two get rather good at it, and licences are bought more widely. A dashboard shows adoption climbing, and everyone takes some comfort from that. Eighteen months later, the company works exactly as it always did, because the work itself never changed, only the tool people used to do it. What follows is a method for changing the work.

Adopting AI tools changes what people use. Redesigning the work around AI changes what a company can do, and that is the part that shows up in the numbers.

02

The practice: how people work day to day

While the engine rebuilds workflows, people's own use of AI has to grow alongside it. Three levels describe that, and most of an organisation should end up at the second.

L1 · Assisted

The starting line

Here, the AI produces a first draft and the person edits it, so it becomes less a blank page and more a thinking partner to argue with. Everyone in the organisation should reach a confident L1 fairly quickly.

L2 · Delegated

Where most people should live

Whole tasks are handed over and reviewed afterwards, from within a saved workspace that already has the right context loaded into it. It's here that most of the model's mistakes tend to get caught.

L3 · Orchestrated

Power users, function champions

At this level, people design multi-step workflows that run with proper checkpoints built in, build reusable components that others go on to use, and mentor those still working their way up from L1.

03

Earning autonomy: four stages before it acts unattended

Every workflow passes through the same four stages. Capability and risk rise together, on the same dial, so the job is to turn that dial deliberately, one step at a time, on the strength of the evidence, instead of trying to reduce the risk to zero.

Capability unlockedRisk if unmanaged
1 · Watch

Shadow

The system runs in the background on live data, but nobody acts on what it produces yet. Its job is to show that it behaves as people would have.

Moves on when its agreement with human judgement has settled, and any disagreements have been understood, not just explained away.
2 · Recommend

Assisted

A recommendation is now produced, with the evidence behind it, but a person still makes the decision.

Moves on when people rarely need to correct it, that rate is still falling and, just as importantly, the people using it every day trust it.
3 · Act, bounded

Delegated, within bounds

The system acts on its own, but only within defined limits: value limits, particular case types, and confidence thresholds below which it will not proceed. Anything outside goes to a person instead of being guessed at.

Moves on when exception handling is proven and the escalation route has held up under real load, not only in a test environment.
4 · Widen on evidence

Extended authority

From here, the limits are widened gradually, as evidence builds that it is safe to do so.

Kept honest by reviews against measured performance throughout, never simply because time has passed since the last one.

05

The Rule of Two: is this safe to run unattended?

Tick the properties a piece of work would have. The verdict, and an example from the industry chosen above, update as you go.

It is worth being clear about one thing: a language model cannot reliably tell a real instruction from its user apart from one hidden in something it is reading, such as a stray line of text on a web page or an email dressed up as something else. The major AI labs say so themselves, and none of them describe it as solved. Where the model runs changes who can see the data and what it all costs. It changes nothing about this risk.

What works is containment, sometimes called the Rule of Two. An AI session can reasonably run unattended if it has no more than two of three properties: access to private data, exposure to content it did not write, and the ability to act or communicate with the outside world. Give it all three at once, unsupervised, and you have built exactly the thing a security team loses sleep over.

06

Principles: what holds it together

Three architectural commitments, and four of the beliefs underneath them.

01

A deterministic core, with models kept to the edges. Language models are used to interpret, draft and triage. Calculations, transactions and records of decisions are kept in deterministic code, which behaves the same way every time and so can be replayed and audited. That is what makes a system built partly on unpredictable AI explainable to a risk function, when the question is asked (and it always is).

02

Decisions become precedent. Every approved judgement is saved as a reusable precedent, so the system becomes more capable through use, as well as through further development.

03

No vendor becomes the system of record. Context, skills, evaluation sets and traces are all kept in formats the organisation owns. The choice of model therefore stays a decision that can be revisited for each workflow, on the evidence, instead of being locked in from the start.

Four of the beliefs underneath it
Value over hype

What matters is hours given back, lead times cut and error rates reduced. Seats issued and prompts run measure activity, not value. If the value cannot be named and shown against a proper baseline, the work has not been done yet.

Working software beats slideware

A prototype that runs on the organisation's own data, built in weeks, tends to settle debates that a hundred slides never could, not least because it is put in front of the people who will have to live with the result.

Start small, earn the right to scale

The destination is described; only the next step is committed to. Each phase is a real decision gate, and the first one is kept cheap enough that stopping there would cost almost nothing.

Augment judgement, don't replace it

AI does the assembly work and the first pass; the decisions with real consequences are still made by people. For that reason, confidence thresholds and escalation routes are designed in from day one, instead of being added after something has gone wrong.

"Where a model runs decides who can see the data and what it all costs. How the system is designed decides what can go wrong, and that is the part that gets my attention."The sentence I ask a board to remember

The build

From backlog to working software

The method above decides what is worth building. This is how it then gets built. I lead product, and when I build software, AI is the engineering team. I run it the way I would run a human one: a ranked backlog, short sprints, a definition of ready, tests before code, an independent review, and a retrospective that leaves nothing stranded. The speed is real, but it isn't the interesting part. The discipline is, because AI makes good practice much faster to follow and just as much faster to skip.

The team: every role a human team would have, each one played deliberately

A single AI asked to do everything will plan, build and mark its own homework. So the roles are separated, as they would be in a well-run team, and the recurring ceremonies are written down as reusable skills, so that every project runs them the same way and nothing depends on remembering how it was done last time.

Product owner

Owns the why, and the two gates

I write the job stories, rank the backlog and decide. Nothing is built until I have agreed the refined stories, and nothing new starts until I have confirmed the next sprint.

Product owner
Tech lead, in refinement

Turns stories into build-ready work

A refinement skill takes the next sprint's stories and adds numbered test cases, risks, out-of-scope notes, open questions (each with a recommended answer) and a size.

Engineers

Claude, as an AI coding agent, writes the agreed tests and then the code that makes them pass. I read and question every change, and every file is checked on disk rather than taken on trust.

AI · coding agent
Code review and QA

Checked by something that didn't build it

A separate agent, which has not seen the work being produced, reviews the code before each delivery. Each finding is fixed, or accepted with the reason written down.

Independent agent
Security and red team

Plays the attacker, on a schedule

Hostile inputs are designed against the system every quarter: hidden instructions, fake recruiters, scam offers. The pass mark is set before the results are in, and every bug found becomes a backlog row.

Independent agent

The sprint cycle: short sprints, each one ending in working software
  1. Backlog
    Product owner

    Job stories in plain words, grouped by stage of the job and ranked by value and risk. The never list is written before any of them.

  2. Refine
    AI · refinement

    The next sprint only, just in time. Tests for normal, edge, failure and hostile cases; risks; questions; size.

  3. Agree
    Product owner · gate

    Questions answered. A story is ready only when its tests are agreed and its boundaries checked. Then go.

  4. Build
    AI · coding agent

    Tests first, then code. Documentation is updated in the same sprint, not saved up for later.

  5. Check
    Independent agent

    Reviews code it did not write. Findings are fixed, or accepted with a reason.

  6. Review
    Both

    Judged on a live run with real data, not only on its tests, because real use finds what tests cannot.

  7. Close
    AI · close skill

    The retrospective. Done or returned, findings logged, backlog re-ranked, next sprint proposed for sign-off.

  8. Back to 1. Unfinished work and every review finding return to the backlog, which is re-ranked before the next sprint is confirmed.

Spikes

Where something can't be known without trying it, a time-boxed spike answers the question instead of a guess. A story that depends on a spike never shares the spike's sprint: it waits for the next one, and is refined at the spike's close on what was actually learned. Putting the spike first in the same sprint only hides the dependency.

Four habits that hold the cycle together
Start with the job, not the feature

Every build starts by agreeing how success will be measured, the constraints, and what the system must never do. "Find the emails about my applications, and only those" is a job; "build an email filter" is not. The first sprint is a thin slice through every stage of the job, so something genuinely usable exists straight away; later sprints deepen it.

Just in time, never too early

Only the next sprint is refined in detail. Later work stays as one-line stories with acceptance criteria, because detail written too early tends to go stale at the very next review.

Nothing stranded

Part-finished work is split: the finished part closes and the rest returns to the backlog. Returned items are re-scored on the same terms as everything else, not moved to the front by default. Nothing raised at a review is allowed to live only in the review notes.

In proportion

A half-day item gets a short note, not a design document. A personal tool gets an afternoon of red teaming; a system that touches customers or money gets a great deal more. The rigour scales with what could go wrong.

Testing: nothing is reported as working until it has been seen to work
01
Test cases agreed in refinement

Every acceptance criterion becomes numbered cases: normal, edge, failure and hostile input. They are agreed before a line of code is written.

The hostile cases matter most for AI: any text written by someone else is treated as data, never as instructions.

02
Automated tests, written first

The build has to make the agreed tests pass, and a fix arrives with a test that failed before it went in.

244 automated tests across Job Scout's code and its checking tools by version 0.12.

03
Independent code review

A separate agent that did not write the code reviews it before each delivery.

Before one release it found eleven issues: nine fixed, two accepted with reasons recorded.

04
AI measured against bars set in advance

Every AI component has a pass mark agreed before it is tested, so a disappointing result can't be argued away afterwards.

The local advert reader had to get requirements and pay right for nine adverts in ten before it was used. It managed 21 of 22.

05
Red team, every quarter

Deliberate attempts to break the system, across the whole of it, not only the AI.

In the task app, the one high-severity finding was ordinary web security. Thirteen issues found, all thirteen fixed and retested.

06
The live run

Each sprint is closed against real use, because AI systems often fail where they meet the real world, not in their reasoning.

Daily use surfaced a power-saving mode that blocked the morning wake-up, and a graphics setting that ran the local model at a tenth of its speed. Both are fixed and in the runbook.

The paper trail: kept with the code, updated at every sprint close

Every project leaves behind the documents a reviewer, an auditor or a new team member would ask for. They are written as the work happens, so the reasoning survives the conversation it came from.
