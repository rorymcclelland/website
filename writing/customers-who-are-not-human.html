Designing for customers who aren’t human

Amazon's chief executive says outside shopping agents are 'still not great'. Meta says ten million people have set one up. Every product team needs to decide how it treats a customer that is software.

Rory McClellandOctober 20264 min read

On Amazon’s earnings call this week, Andy Jassy said outside AI shopping agents are “still not great, nor more convenient than coming to a retailer directly”. Amazon blocked Meta’s Muse assistant from its store in September, citing its terms of use, and Jassy said few customers buy that way yet. He pointed instead to Amazon’s own shopping agent, which he said more than 400 million customers used in the past year.

The same week, Mark Zuckerberg said more than ten million people in the US and Canada have set up Muse, that shopping is its most common use, and that the average purchase is above $200. Shopify, Walmart, and Best Buy have welcomed it.

Both men are talking their book, and both are probably right about something. Today’s agents are clumsy. Plenty of people use them anyway. For a product team, the useful question is what you do when the visitor on the other side of your page is a piece of software acting for someone.

I build the visitor

I see this from the agent’s side. Job Scout, my job-search agent, visits other people’s websites every morning to read job adverts, and I had to decide how it should behave as a guest.

It tries a job board’s official interface first, where one exists. It looks for the advert on the employer’s own careers page before an aggregator’s copy, because the aggregators were often wrong about whether a role was still open. It respects robots.txt. It never touches LinkedIn, which does not want automated readers. Some sites turn it away altogether: Microsoft’s full jobs list is closed to automated readers, so the agent reports that instead of trying to get round it.

Those rules cost me coverage. I accepted that, because an agent that ignores a site’s wishes is one the site will eventually block, and it would deserve to be.

Decisions for the other side

If you run the site, the shop, or the service, an agent customer forces decisions that most teams have not yet made on purpose.

  1. Who is the customer? The person who wants the outcome, or the agent acting for them? The answer changes who you design for, who you support, and who you can sell to.
  2. What can an agent do without the person confirming? Browsing and comparing are low risk. Payment, cancellation, and anything irreversible are not. Job Scout can find, score, and draft, but it has no way to send an application. I sign off every one, and a retailer can draw the same line at the checkout.
  3. How do you know it has authority? A human shopper proves who they are by logging in. An agent needs a way to show who it acts for and what it is allowed to spend. Without that, every agent looks like a scraper or a fraudster.
  4. What does your page say to an agent? Prices, stock, delivery dates, and returns policies that a person reads from a well-designed page, an agent needs in a form it can parse reliably. If it cannot, it will guess, and its user will blame you.

Blocking is a strategy with a shelf life

Amazon can block outside agents because it is the destination. Customers start their search there, and Amazon has its own agent to offer them instead. That is a real advantage, and a choice very few retailers can copy.

For everyone else, blocking only works while customers keep coming to you directly. If a growing share of them start with an agent, the shop that turns agents away is choosing to be invisible to those customers. A better default is to welcome agents for the low-risk parts of the journey, keep a person in the loop for the irreversible steps, and decide your terms before the traffic arrives.

Design for both

It is fair to say we are early. Jassy is right that most agent shopping today is worse than doing it yourself, and Zuckerberg’s ten million set-ups are not ten million regular shoppers. But product decisions take longer than market shifts, and teams that design for agent customers now will be ready when the clumsy phase ends.

So add a persona to your next discovery round: the agent acting for your customer. Ask what it needs, what it should never be allowed to do, and how your customer would know it did the right thing.
