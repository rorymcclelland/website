The method should outlast the consultant

The most valuable thing an AI programme can leave behind is a way of thinking the organisation can run for itself.

Rory McClellandSeptember 20263 min read

There is a familiar pattern to AI programmes in large organisations. A team arrives, identifies a set of processes, builds agents for the most promising ones and rolls them out. Some of them work. Then the team leaves, and the judgement that picked those processes, scored them and decided what was safe leaves with it. The next opportunity waits for the next engagement.

There has been plenty of coverage this year of agent pilots that stall before they reach production. In my experience the model is rarely the cause. More often, nobody inside the organisation owns the result, the data or the decision about what the agent may do, and nobody has been shown how to make those calls.

I started as an engineer and moved into product, so I have spent most of my career between the people who decide what to build and the people who build it. Working with AI has convinced me that the most valuable thing to hand over at the end of a programme is the method itself.

What the thinking looks like

The method I use is a loop: find opportunities, keep a live and scored backlog, deliver the top item, fold what was built into a shared foundation, then go round again. Four habits sit at the heart of it, and none of them needs a consultant once it has been learnt.

Start with the work. Opportunities are found by looking at the work through five lenses: the coordination tax of chasing, status updates and hand-offs; the time spent assembling context before anyone can start; judgement bottlenecks, where decisions queue on one person's desk; the queues the business already complains about; and work that has never been tried because it needed too many people. That last one rarely appears on anyone's list, and it is often the biggest win.

Every gate is allowed to say no. There are four gates: is it worth a pilot, do we fund the pilot, does it go to production, and do we extend it. Before scoring, each candidate must pass five tests: it can ship in four to six weeks, it leaves something others can reuse, a baseline to measure against exists today, the data and access can be secured in week one, and someone senior owns the result. A team that has never seen an idea stopped at a gate does not yet believe the gates are real.

Each step up has a named owner and a tested way back.

Authority is earned. An agent moves up in steps, and only on evidence:

Each lap should cost less than the last. What one pilot builds (business context, evaluations, the way agents are given an identity and permissions) is folded into a foundation the next pilot can draw on. My test for this is blunt: if lap two costs what lap one did, stop and find out why.

Practising each role with AI

The second half of the idea is more personal. With AI tools, one person can now work through each of the roles involved in building a product. There is the engineer's question (can it be built, and how will it fail?), the product manager's (who is it for, and what is it worth?) and the executive's (should we fund it, and what could it cost us?). The AI covers the skills you lack in each role, which leaves the thinking for you to practise.

That is how I build my own tools now. I lead product, Claude is the engineering team, and I run it the way I would run a human one, with a ranked backlog, short sprints, tests before code and a never list agreed before anything is built. It is also why I think this way of thinking can be taught to people across an organisation, at any level of seniority. Someone who has taken one small idea all the way through, safely, makes better decisions about the next hundred.

What I would aim to leave behind

For a leadership team, the outcome worth paying for is an organisation that can:

  • find and score its own opportunities, and publish the scores;
  • stop an idea at a gate without embarrassment;
  • give an agent more authority only on evidence; and
  • make its second pilot cheaper than its first.

Systems will be built along the way, and they matter. The habit is what compounds.
