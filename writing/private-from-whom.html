Private from whom?

Why “I want privacy” is not yet a requirement, and the two questions that turn it into one.

Rory McClellandOctober 20264 min read

I was Chrome's first privacy product manager at Google. The sentence I heard most often in that job, from users, researchers and colleagues alike, was some version of "people want privacy". It is true. On its own, though, it tells you almost nothing about what to build.

The lesson I took from that work, and still apply to everything I build with AI, is that privacy only becomes a requirement once two more questions have been answered. Private from whom? And whom is the person willing to trust to get it?

One feature, two opposite answers

A problem from my time on Chrome shows why the questions matter.

When someone browses in Incognito and visits a site that uses Google's services, a cookie is set and a session can be opened on Google's servers. When the person closes their last Incognito window, Chrome deletes everything on the device, as promised. The server is never told. From its side, the session simply goes quiet and lives on until it expires, so a regular Incognito user can leave a long trail of orphaned sessions behind them.

The obvious fix is for the browser to tell the server when an Incognito session ends, so the server can clean up after itself. Whether that is a privacy improvement depends entirely on whom the user is worried about.

Then comes the question of the default. If you cannot tell which kind of user you have, the cautious choice is to leave the feature off and let people opt in. Very few people change a default, however, so an opt-in privacy feature protects very few people, and "we built it, but didn't turn it on" is a hard thing to announce. Legal, privacy, engineering and communications each held a reasonable position, and the positions could not all be satisfied at once. That is what a real privacy trade-off looks like.

Why AI makes the question sharper

Each ring is a different design, built in a different part of the product.

An AI assistant holds far more than a browsing session. A single conversation might contain someone's health worries, their finances and the names of their colleagues. "Private" could mean private from any of these:

  • other people who use the same device or account;
  • the websites and services the assistant reaches on the person's behalf;
  • the company providing the assistant, and anyone it shares data with;
  • anyone who could later compel that company to hand data over;
  • the model itself, if conversations are used to train future versions.

Protection from the person at the next desk is a question for the interface. Protection from the provider is a question of architecture: where the data is processed, and what is kept. Protection from a court order comes down to what exists to be handed over at all. A team that writes "the product must be private" in its requirements has not yet made any of these decisions.

How the question shaped a build of my own

When I built Job Scout, an automated job search that runs every morning on my own mailbox, this was the first question I asked. I am willing to trust the cloud model that drafts my CVs with my own career notes. I was much less willing to send it other people's emails, or adverts written by strangers that might carry hidden instructions.

So the mailbox is read only by code on the local PC, which can read mail but cannot change or send anything. Adverts and replies are read by a small model that also runs on the local PC, with no tools and no internet access, and only a checked summary ever leaves the machine. The answer to "private from whom?" decided the architecture before any feature was designed.

Three questions for any product team

  1. Private from whom? List every party the user might want privacy from, then rank them by what the user fears most.
  2. Whom will the user trust to get it? Every privacy feature asks the user to trust someone, even if that someone is only their own device.
  3. What happens by default? For most users the default is the product. If the right default depends on which user you have, say so, and make the trade-off deliberately, with a named owner, written down where the next person can find it.

For most people, privacy is a hygiene factor. They will rarely choose a product because of it, but they will leave one that gets it wrong. That makes it easy to under-invest in and expensive to repair, and asking "private from whom?" early is the cheapest way I know of getting it right.
