Goals follow the view

What a person can see of the business tends to decide the goals they are able to write. AI makes that matter more, because building is no longer the slow part.

Rory McClellandNovember 20263 min read

Most advice on goal setting is about the wording. Make the goal specific, give it a number, and tie it to a date. I think that starts one step too late. What each person can see of the business significantly affects the goals they are able to write. A narrow view leads to goals about activity; a wide view makes it possible to choose the right problem.

A goal is a guess about what matters

Take a team lead in customer support (an invented example, though a familiar one). They can see their own queue, so they set a goal to cut reply times by a fifth. What they cannot see is the product data showing that a third of those tickets come from one confusing screen. The goal is sensible from where they sit. It is also aimed at the wrong problem, and rewording it would not help.

Why AI raises the stakes

When building was slow, the cost of a poorly chosen goal was limited by how little could be built in a quarter. AI coding tools and agents have removed much of that limit, so a team can now deliver the wrong thing quickly and in volume. Choosing the problem becomes the scarce skill, however it depends on context most people are never given: customer evidence, the numbers, and why past decisions were made.

What it tends to look like

The signs are usually easy to spot once you look for them:

  • Goals that describe activity (‘ship X’) and say little about the problem behind it.
  • Goals that rarely surprise the person’s manager, because they were written to be approved.
  • Different people describing the same issue in different ways.

Context first, then goals

Before changing how goals are written or reviewed, I would give everyone the same view: what customers are saying, the handful of numbers that matter, and past decisions with their reasons. I would then ask each person for one page before any work starts, covering the problem, the evidence for it, who it matters to, and how we would know it had gone. AI can help by challenging that page with questions drawn from the shared context. The choice itself should stay with a person.

This is also where I would start an AI programme. A workflow is only worth redesigning once it is clear whose problem it solves.

A cheap test

All of this is a hypothesis, and a cheap one to test. Ask ten people what the most important problem in front of them is, and what evidence tells them so. Then ask their managers the same question about them. The gap between the two sets of answers may show which view is missing.
