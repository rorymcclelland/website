What your open tabs say about you

A small Firefox add-on sorts my tabs into groups with AI. The model runs on the local PC, so one of the most revealing lists on my computer never leaves it.

Rory McClellandOctober 20264 min read

I keep far too many tabs open. On a typical day they are a mix of documentation, job adverts, half-read articles, something I meant to buy and at least one thing I opened by mistake. Firefox's tab groups help, but sorting them all by hand is exactly the kind of chore I would rather hand to a machine.

HayaTab is a Firefox add-on that does that one job. You click Analyse Tabs, it suggests a set of named groups, and you review them before anything moves. Once you apply them, it uses Firefox's own tab groups.

To decide on the groups it needs a language model, and it offers four: Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini through your own API key, or Ollama, which runs open models on your own machine. I use Ollama.

Your tabs are your browsing history

I was Chrome's first privacy product manager at Google, and that job made it very clear how much a browser knows about the person using it. A list of open tabs is browsing history at its most current: what you are working on, what you are worried about, who you are interviewing with, what you are about to buy and, quite often, what you have just searched for, because search terms tend to sit in the address.

Sending that list to a cloud model sends a snapshot of your day to a third party, every time you tidy up. The providers HayaTab supports are reputable, and each has its own terms on retention and training. It is still one more copy of very personal data, held somewhere else, in exchange for a small convenience.

A small convenience is a poor reason to share a very personal list.

In Private from whom? I argued that privacy only becomes a requirement once you say whom it is from. Here the answer is easy: the provider, and anyone who could ever compel the provider to hand data over. A model on the local PC deals with both, because the list never leaves the machine.

Read what the add-on can reach

Before installing anything that reads your tabs, it is worth a minute on its listing. HayaTab asks for access to your tabs and to exactly four addresses: the Anthropic, OpenAI and Google APIs, and localhost, which is where Ollama listens. That is a short, specific list, and a good sign, because it names every place the add-on expects to send your tabs.

The listing also declares 'browsing activity' as data the add-on collects. That sounds alarming, but it is the honest consequence of an add-on that reads your tabs, and where that data goes depends on the model you choose. The code is open source under the MIT licence, so it can be checked. The add-on is also very new, with only a handful of users so far, which is a reason to read the permissions carefully (and a good habit for any add-on, however popular).

Slower, free and good enough

The cloud option is already cheap, at a fraction of a cent per analysis according to the listing, so cost is not really the argument here. Running it locally is free.

The trade-off is speed. Ollama loads a model into memory when it is first asked for one, so the first analysis of a session waits for that, and by default it unloads the model again after five minutes of inactivity, so the memory comes back once the tabs are sorted. In use it is a little slower than a cloud model, and the groups are just as good.

That is no coincidence. Sorting a list of titles into a few sensible groups is a small, well-defined job, and small, well-defined jobs over personal data are exactly where local models already hold their own. That makes them the best place to start with local AI.

The same reasoning has replaced the dictation app I used to use, which I have written up in Keep your voice on your own machine.
