Sovereign isn’t the same as local

Mistral has released the weights for a trillion-parameter model, pitched as defence nobody can switch off. Open weights give you control of the model. Where your data goes is a separate decision, and usually the more important one.

Rory McClellandOctober 20264 min read

Mistral has put the weights for Mistral Large 4 on Hugging Face. It is a trillion-parameter multimodal model (about 52 billion parameters appear to be active at once), released under a modified MIT licence that allows commercial use, with a separate licence needed only to sell it as a hosted service. Mistral is pitching it as “sovereign cyber defence” for companies and governments. Its VP of Science, Pierre Stock, told TechCrunch that with open weights, access “can’t be cut off”.

That is a real benefit, and one I take seriously. It is also easy to blur with a different benefit, which is keeping your data close. They are not the same thing, and the difference shapes how you should build.

Two questions hiding in one word

‘Sovereign’ answers the question “who controls the model?” If you hold the weights, no vendor can withdraw the model, change its behaviour overnight, or raise the price. You can inspect it, fine-tune it, and test it on your own data whenever you like.

‘Local’ answers a different question: “where does the data go?” A sovereign model running in a data centre still receives whatever you send it. A trillion-parameter model is not going to run on a desktop PC. For most organisations, sovereignty at this scale means a rack of GPUs in a facility, which brings its own access controls, staff, and logs.

So an open-weight model can be sovereign without being local, and a small local model can keep data on the machine while being less capable than you would like. Most real systems need both properties, applied to different steps.

Decide by data, step by step

Job Scout, my job-search agent, is built that way. Each kind of data has a tier that decides where it may go:

The local step uses a 14-billion-parameter model that passed my own retest at 21 of 22 adverts. It is nowhere near a frontier model, and it does not need to be. Its job is narrow, and the data it handles is the data I least want to leave the house. The heavy writing goes to the cloud, on data I have already decided may go there.

That is the decision I would push any team to make before choosing a model. List the data. Give each kind a tier. Then pick the smallest model that can do each step within its tier.

The economics are usually against owning the rack

I recently worked through the case for running open-weight models on owned hardware for an organisation of about a hundred people. A company working in one time zone cannot keep its GPUs busy more than about 36% of the time, which roughly triples the real cost per token compared with the published break-even analyses. And its likely volume sat four to ten times below the point where self-hosting starts to pay. Sovereignty was worth something, but not that much.

The arithmetic will be different for a government, a bank, or a security team, which is exactly who Mistral is aiming at. For most organisations, though, renting frontier capability for the heavy steps and owning a small model for the sensitive ones is the better trade.

Open weights move the guardrails to you

There is one more thing the sovereignty pitch implies. Mistral reports strong safety figures: a 96.6% refusal rate on malicious cyber requests and 97.1% resistance on an agent security benchmark. It also reports seeing “a potential for uplift to conduct real attacks” during the preview, and says it is modifying its guardrails in response.

With a hosted model, the vendor can change those guardrails for everyone. With open weights, anyone can fine-tune them away, and the guardrails that protect you have to live in your own system: the permissions, the input checks, and the never list. Sovereignty hands you the controls along with the responsibility.

The question to ask

When someone pitches a model as sovereign, ask who controls the model, and where your data will go. Then decide each step of your system on its own answers, and test the model you choose on your own data before you trust it with any of them.
