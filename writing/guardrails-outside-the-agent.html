Guardrails the agent can’t talk its way past

Nvidia's new agent safety platform puts the guard outside the agent. It is the right instinct, and you do not need a data centre to apply it.

Rory McClellandOctober 20264 min read

Nvidia’s Open Agent Safety Platform has two halves. OpenShell is open-source software that runs an agent inside a boundary, traces what it does, and enforces a policy as it works. Sentry runs on a separate chip, outside the agent’s own environment, and can quarantine an agent within milliseconds if it tries to step outside that boundary. More than 100 organisations are said to be working with it, and Tim Bajarin’s Forbes column this week called the location of the enforcement the key design choice.

The line I keep coming back to is from one of the launch partners, SpaceXAI: safety “should be enforced outside the model by additional controls the agent can’t get past”. That is the whole argument in one sentence, and it applies to a hobby project on a laptop as much as to a rack of Nvidia hardware.

A prompt is a request

Most guardrails I see in early agent builds live in the system prompt. “Never share the customer’s address.” “Do not send anything without approval.” They read well in a demo, and they are written in the same language the attacker uses. A web page, an email, or a document the agent reads can carry instructions of its own, and the model has no reliable way to tell your rules from theirs. That is what prompt injection is.

So a rule in a prompt is a request to the model. A rule in the code, the permissions, or the infrastructure is a control. The model can be persuaded to ignore the first. It cannot be persuaded to use a capability it was never given.

If the honest answer is “in the prompt”, it is a hope.

None of those depends on a model behaving well. If the advert reader were fully compromised by a hostile page, the worst it could do is return a wrong answer in a fixed shape, which the next step checks. It has nothing to act with. That is the same idea as Nvidia’s, scaled down to a single PC.

I am not alone in landing here. monday.com published how its infrastructure agents now resolve about half of its infrastructure tickets without a person stepping in. The striking detail is that none of its 11 guardrails relies on the agent behaving: short-lived tokens scoped to the right repositories, no generic shell tool, a separate model that scores every change, kill switches, and humans still merging every change.

The hardware is optional

The Forbes piece is fair about the caveats. A hundred partners is not a hundred production deployments, and a design that depends on specialist chips raises cost questions for mid-sized companies. I would add that out-of-band hardware is the last layer to add. Most teams will get further, sooner, by removing capabilities their agents do not need.

It is fair to say the principle is the valuable part. Put the enforcement where the agent cannot reach it, and decide that before the build starts, because retrofitting it later means taking capabilities away from something people already rely on.

One question for your own build

Take your never list, or write one, and add the second column. For each line, ask where it is enforced. If the honest answer is “in the prompt”, it is a hope. Move it into the permissions, the code, or the infrastructure, or accept the risk with your eyes open and write down who signed it off.

The agent can be clever, persuasive, and wrong. It should still be unable to do the things on the list.
