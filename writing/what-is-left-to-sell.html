What’s left to sell when the seat goes

ServiceNow had its worst day ever on a quarter in which subscription revenue grew 20%. The market is pricing in build versus buy, and the vendors are already moving to sell control.

Rory McClellandOctober 20264 min read

On Thursday, ServiceNow’s shares fell about 27%, the worst day in the company’s history. The quarter behind it was not bad. Subscription revenue grew 20.5% in constant currency, and contracted revenue for the next twelve months grew 21%. What spooked investors was guidance that next quarter’s growth would slow to 18.5%, and a headline about “AI disruption fears”. By Friday’s close the shares were down about 34% on the week, with Salesforce, Workday, and Adobe all lower too.

In The approximation tax I argued that buying software means paying for the vendor’s approximation of your process, and that building is now cheap enough to reconsider. I did not expect the stock market to make the point quite so loudly a few weeks later. Markets overshoot, and one quarter’s guidance proves very little. The more interesting story is in what ServiceNow said on the call.

The seat was a proxy for a person

For most of the SaaS era, the seat worked as a unit because a seat was a person doing the work. I saw that from the inside at North Plains, where I owned On Brand, a SaaS brand asset management product. More people meant more value, more seats, and more revenue, and everyone could budget for it.

Agents break that link. If an agent does the triage, drafts the response, and closes the ticket, the number of people logging in stops tracking the value delivered. On the call, Bill McDermott said customers still like “the predictability of the seat-based pricing model” for budgeting, with consumption growing on top as they go live. He talked about revenue per user rising as much as five times. The CFO, Gina Mastantuono, said customers “are not asking at all for outcome pricing”. That is a hybrid model, and it is an honest picture of a market in transition. It is also a sign that the seat is now a budgeting convenience more than a measure of value.

The pivot to control

The part of the call that caught my attention was Control Tower. Amit Zavery described it as “not about orchestration really”, and said it is about visibility, control, and safety. Management said it can revoke an agent’s access across partner platforms.

That is a vendor telling you where it thinks the durable value sits. The workflow, the screens, and the forms are becoming the cheap part. The expensive part is knowing what every agent is doing, being able to stop it, and proving afterwards that it behaved.

That matches what I find when I build. The code an AI coding agent writes for me is quick and cheap. What takes the time is everything around it. My RFI tool has 874 automated tests, an independent checker that judged 305 of 305 claims in its evaluation supported by the source, and a measured line for bulk approval with 0 of 33 near misses above it. None of that is the feature. All of it is why anyone could trust the feature.

Build the approximation, buy the assurance

For a product team weighing build against buy, first split what you would be paying for into two parts. One is the workflow: the screens, the forms, and the process logic that approximates how you work. The other is the assurance: audit trails, access control, evaluation, monitoring, and someone accountable when it goes wrong.

Second, be honest about which part is hard for you. A small team with AI coding tools can now build a workflow that fits its process better than most off-the-shelf products. Building the assurance well is slower, duller, and much easier to get wrong.

Third, buy where the assurance is the product. If a vendor’s real value is governance you could not easily reproduce, pay for it, and negotiate on that basis. If it is mostly a workflow you have outgrown, you are paying the approximation tax, and it is worth asking whether you still need to.

The question for every SaaS renewal

ServiceNow has a large installed base and a credible move into agent governance, so the sharper question is for buyers. At your next renewal, ask what the seats are paying for. If the answer is “people using the screens”, count how many of those people an agent will replace. If the answer is “the controls we could not build ourselves”, you may have found the part worth keeping.
